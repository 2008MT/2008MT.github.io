Why Do Clocks Move Like the Sun?

A near-perfect correlation, and no causation

Mael Tok

The prompt Pick a famous spurious correlation, and make the case that it might reveal a deeper, causal truth.

I have long loved watches. For their beauty, their art of craftsmanship, their symbol, their mechanics; but essentially for their indispensability and the quiet audacity of what they do: tell time. Years ago, staring at the slow, deliberate motion of a clock’s hands, I asked my grandfather—a former physics professor obsessed with mechanics and simply lifelong learning—a question that seemed naïve: Why do the hands move exactly like the Sun? His answer stayed with me far longer than I expected.

For most of human history, time was not measured—it was observed. The Sun rose, shadows shifted, and days passed. Ancient Egyptian sundials, used as early as 1500 BCE, translated the Sun’s motion directly into time: when the shadow moved, the hour advanced. In this system, causation was obvious. The Sun moved; time followed.

What is striking is that, thousands of years later, clocks still move in near-perfect synchrony with the Sun. As the Sun climbs, clock hands advance. Around midday, they point upright. Statistically, the correlation is almost perfect. One could easily conclude that the Sun still drives the clock.

It does not.

The persistence of this correlation is not natural—it is engineered. When mechanical clocks appeared in medieval Europe in the 13th and 14th centuries, they were calibrated explicitly to replicate solar time. The clock did not replace the Sun; it imitated it. Later refinements—pendulum clocks in the 17th century, quartz oscillators in the 20th—did not weaken this relationship. They reinforced it.

Even the most precise definition of time reflects this legacy. In 1967, the second was redefined using atomic physics: 9,192,631,770 oscillations of a cesium-133 atom. Yet this number was chosen not arbitrarily, but to preserve continuity with the historical solar day of 86,400 seconds. Atomic clocks do not follow the Sun—but they are tuned to remember it.

In Montpellier, where I wrote this essay, the pattern even carries a human signature. The city lies barely east of London's meridian, yet France runs on the same clock time as Berlin: it moved to German time under the occupation in 1940 and never moved back. As a result, the Sun reaches its highest point there not at noon, but around 12:45 in winter and 13:45 in summer. The correlation is still near-perfect; only its offset is political.

At first sight, this looks like a classic spurious correlation: two variables move together with remarkable precision, yet one does not mechanically drive the other. But it is not quite spurious. A spurious correlation requires a hidden common cause, and nothing causes both the Sun and the clock. What links them is a chain: the Sun moves, humans observe it, and centuries of watchmakers, astronomers, and institutions set their machines to match. The Sun still causes the clock—just not directly. It does so through us.

The lesson extends far beyond horology. In finance, some of the most striking regularities are designed rather than discovered. For decades, a stock added to the S&P 500 tended to jump on the announcement, even though nothing about the company had changed: index funds, bound by their own rules, simply had to buy it. The cause was not the company, but the design of the market around it. The danger lies in mistaking such patterns for laws of nature, when the true driver may be structural design: incentives, expectations, regulations, or models themselves shaping behavior.

The clock teaches restraint. Correlation can persist long after the direct cause is gone. Patterns may survive not because nature enforces them, but because humans choose to maintain them.

When patterns seem inevitable, the most important question is not what moves together, but who and what designed it that way.
