Why Do Clocks Move Like the Sun?

A near-perfect correlation, and no causation

Mael Tok

The prompt Statistically speaking, ice cream doesn’t cause shark attacks, pet spending doesn’t drive the number of lawyers in California, and margarine consumption isn’t responsible for Maine’s divorce rate—at least, not according to conventional wisdom. But what if the statisticians got it wrong? Choose your favorite spurious correlation and make the case for why it might actually reveal a deeper, causative truth. University of Chicago essay prompt, inspired by Adam DiMascio, Class of 2025

I have long loved watches. For their beauty, their art of craftsmanship, their symbol, their mechanics; but essentially for their indispensability and the quiet audacity of what they do: tell time. Years ago, staring at the slow, deliberate motion of a clock’s hands, I asked my grandfather—a former physics professor obsessed with mechanics and simply lifelong learning—a question that seemed naïve: Why do the hands move exactly like the Sun? His answer stayed with me far longer than I expected.

For most of human history, time was not measured—it was observed. The Sun rose, shadows shifted, and days passed. Ancient Egyptian sundials, used as early as 1500 BCE, translated the Sun’s motion directly into time: when the shadow moved, the hour advanced. In this system, causation was obvious. The Sun moved; time followed.

What is striking is that, thousands of years later, clocks still move in near-perfect synchrony with the Sun. As the Sun climbs, clock hands advance. At solar noon, they align upright. Statistically, the correlation is almost perfect. One could easily conclude that the Sun still drives the clock.

It does not.

The persistence of this correlation is not natural—it is engineered. When mechanical clocks appeared in medieval Europe in the 13th and 14th centuries, they were calibrated explicitly to replicate solar time. The clock did not replace the Sun; it imitated it. Later refinements—pendulum clocks in the 17th century, quartz oscillators in the 20th—did not weaken this relationship. They reinforced it.

Even the most precise definition of time reflects this legacy. In 1967, the second was redefined using atomic physics: 9,192,631,770 oscillations of a cesium-133 atom. Yet this number was chosen not arbitrarily, but to preserve continuity with the historical solar day of 86,400 seconds. Atomic clocks do not follow the Sun—but they are tuned to remember it.

This creates a classic spurious correlation. Two variables move together with remarkable precision, yet one does not cause the other. The hidden variable is human intention: centuries of watchmakers, astronomers, and institutions aligning mechanical systems to a celestial reference long after the causal link disappeared.

The lesson extends far beyond horology. In economics and finance, variables often display similarly easy and striking correlations—asset prices track sentiment, volatility rises with uncertainty, cycles appear rhythmic and predictable. The danger lies in assuming direct causation, when the true driver may be structural design: incentives, expectations, regulations, or models themselves shaping behavior.

The clock teaches restraint. Correlation can persist even after causation is gone. Patterns may survive not because nature enforces them, but because humans choose to maintain them.

When patterns seem inevitable, the most important question is not what moves together, but who and what designed it that way.
