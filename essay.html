Machines Read Stories Too

Do Algorithms Amplify Market Narratives?

Mael Tok · McGill University

At 1:07 p.m. on April 23, 2013, the Associated Press's Twitter account, which had just been hacked, published twelve words: “Breaking: Two explosions in the White House and Barack Obama is injured.” Within minutes, the major U.S. indices fell by about 1%, and roughly $136 billion of S&P 500 value disappeared, before prices recovered once the story was denied.1 Nothing about any company had changed. A story had—and machines read it first.

This episode, now known as the “Hack Crash,” embodies a question I wondered about, and wrote an essay about, in high school—and one I now want to revisit, as the advent of AI has only confirmed my thinking: are financial markets, despite their models and their data, still driven by stories? And if so, what happens when most of the readers are no longer human?

Markets can be understood, at least in part, as a form of collective storytelling: millions of participants act on their own interpretation of reality, and the sum of those interpretations becomes a price. The usual objection is that this picture belongs to the past. Today, most trading in U.S. equities is executed by algorithms,2 which do not panic, do not dream and do not read Reddit for entertainment. Markets, the argument goes, should therefore be becoming more rational.

I think the opposite is closer to the truth. Algorithms do not remove narratives from markets; they inherit them, accelerate them and, increasingly, create them. They make story-driven price moves faster and sharper—even if, as I will argue at the end, often shorter. They do so through at least three channels.

The idea that stories move prices is not new. Robert Shiller has called it “narrative economics”: stories that spread like epidemics and change how people spend, save and invest.3 From tulip bulbs in 1637 to the margin-fuelled optimism of the late 1920s, when the same promise of endless growth was told again and believed again, we can recall many examples.

I met this phenomenon in my own research. Studying Arafura Rare Earths, an Australian mine developer, I found that the stock's largest gains came from policy, not company news: in 2024, its shares rose 76% in a single day when the Australian government announced A$840 million in funding for the mine, and its 2019 rally came as China threatened to curb rare-earth exports.4 The mine's neodymium–praseodymium reserve report had not changed. The story about economic security had.

The most direct channel is the simplest. Many trading systems now scan news wires and social media and turn words into orders in milliseconds. In the Hack Crash, analysts described high-frequency systems that treated combinations such as “White House” and “explosion” as high-risk signals and sold without any human intervention. No trader had time to ask whether the AP's account had been hacked, or to pause at the idea that news of the president being injured would be announced so simply, and so fast, in a single post on Twitter. The machines did not believe the story the way a human would. They simply acted on it, faster than anyone could doubt it.

The second channel is less visible. Many systematic strategies respond to momentum, volume and volatility—quantities that human narratives produce. A story pushes retail investors to buy; their buying moves the price; the move triggers trend-following signals; those signals buy as well, and the higher price makes the original story look true. A feedback loop forms, in which the algorithm does not need to understand the narrative to amplify it.

GameStop in January 2021 is the clearest recent example. A story spread on Reddit—that the company was undervalued and that hedge funds betting against it could be made to pay—and the stock rose by about 2,700% in three weeks, to an intraday high of $483. The popular explanation was a short squeeze. Yet the SEC's staff report later concluded that buying to cover short positions was only a small fraction of total buying, and that “it was the positive sentiment, not the buying-to-cover, that sustained the weeks-long price appreciation.”5

The third channel worries regulators most. When many models are trained on similar data and react to similar signals, they can make the same decision at the same moment. In its October 2024 Global Financial Stability Report, the IMF noted that AI-driven ETFs turn over their holdings far more often than traditional actively managed funds, and that some of them traded more during the March 2020 turmoil, “indicating potential for increased herd-like selling during times of stress.” It warned of “amplified selling in times of stress due to rapid, correlated AI-driven decision-making.”6

A single human investor who panics barely moves the market. A group of algorithms reaching the same conclusion acts like one giant trader. It breaks a core assumption of perfect competition—that no single participant can move the price—and the deeper condition behind efficient prices: that individual mistakes are independent and cancel each other out.

Yet speed cuts both ways: the same algorithms that spread a story can also correct it. The Hack Crash reversed within minutes: as soon as the AP and the White House denied the report, the same speed worked in the other direction, and traders—human and algorithmic—bought back what had just been sold. More generally, many algorithms are built to profit when prices drift away from what fundamentals justify: when a price collapses while earnings, cash flows and the prices of related assets stay unchanged, they buy, and in doing so push it back. In liquid markets with clear fundamentals, a false narrative is an opportunity for someone else's model.

The more accurate conclusion is therefore a narrower one: algorithms make narrative-driven moves faster and sharper, but often shorter. Stories last longest where fundamentals are hardest to pin down—meme stocks, early-stage miners, new technologies—which is exactly where the gap between a price and a value is widest.

This market description is already changing. As AI agents begin to trade, they will read the same headlines, trained on much of the same text, and may converge on the same interpretation of events. AI is also beginning to write some of the news that other AI then reads. The question is no longer only whether human stories move markets, but whether machines will start telling stories to each other—with humans watching the price.

Some markets already put a price on narratives directly. On prediction markets such as Polymarket, a story—“will this conflict escalate?”—becomes a probability that trades in real time.

Markets have always run on stories. What has changed is who reads them first, and how fast the reader acts.
